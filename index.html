Overview

We built a path tracer! Last time we were turning triangles into pixels, and this time we go the other way around, shooting rays out of the camera through every pixel and asking what each one sees. Part 1 is generating those rays and figuring out where they hit triangles and spheres. Part 2 makes that fast with a bounding volume hierarchy, because testing every ray against every triangle gets old really quickly. Part 3 is where it starts actually looking like light, with direct lighting done two ways, uniform hemisphere sampling and importance sampling the lights. Part 4 adds bounces so light can hit one surface and then another, which gets us global illumination, and Russian Roulette lets us stop paths randomly without messing up the answer. Part 5 is adaptive sampling, which spends samples on the pixels that are actually hard instead of spreading them evenly.

The most interesting thing I learned is how much noise depends on how you pick your samples really more than how many samples you can handle.. Hemisphere sampling and light sampling are estimating the exact same integral, and they both get to the same answer eventually, but one of them looks like TV static and the other one looks clean at the same sample count. Same math, totally different picture, which is very cool.

Part 1: Ray Generation and Scene Intersection

Ray generation and the intersection pipeline

For every pixel we shoot a bunch of rays out of the camera and average what they see. In raytrace_pixel I pick ns_aa random points inside the pixel, divide each one by the image width and height so it's between 0 and 1, and hand it to generate_ray. In camera space the camera sits at the origin looking down \(-z\), and there's a pretend sensor on the plane \(z = -1\). The sensor goes from \(-\tan(\frac{hFov}{2})\) to \(\tan(\frac{hFov}{2})\) side to side, and the same thing with vFov up and down. So I just stretch my 0 to 1 point onto the sensor:

\[ x_s = (2x - 1)\tan\left(\frac{hFov}{2}\right), \quad y_s = (2y - 1)\tan\left(\frac{vFov}{2}\right) \]

Since the camera is at the origin, the direction to that sensor point is just the point itself, \((x_s, y_s, -1)\). I rotate it into world space with c2w, normalize it, and the ray starts at the camera's position. Unfortunately hFov and vFov are in degrees, so they need to be converted to radians before taking the tan. Then min_t and max_t get set to the near and far clipping planes.Then every primitive gets a shot at the ray. A hit only counts if its \(t\) is between min_t and max_t, and whenever a primitive finds a hit it sets max_t to that \(t\). That way anything after it has to be closer to count, so once everything has been tested the ray is left holding the nearest hit, so the ray basically keeps track of the closest thing on its own and the pixel's color is the average of all its samples.

Triangle intersection

I used the Moller-Trumbore algorithm. The idea is honestly pretty simple once it clicks. A ray is just a starting point plus some distance \(t\) along a direction, and any point inside a triangle can be described as a mix of its three corners. Barycentric coordinates are back from hw1! So "does this ray hit this triangle?" turns into "is there some distance \(t\) where the ray lands on a point that's a mix of the three corners?" That's three equations (one each for x, y, and z) and three unknowns (\(t\) plus two of the corner weights, since the third weight is just whatever's left over to make them add up to 1). So it's really just solving a tiny system of equations, and Moller-Trumbore is a fast way to do that with a couple of cross products and dot products instead of building and inverting a whole matrix.

Once I have the answer, I check whether it actually counts. If the ray is parallel to the triangle there's no solution at all, so no hit. If any of the corner weights comes out negative, or they add up to more than 1, the ray hit the flat plane the triangle sits on but landed outside the triangle itself, so no hit. And if \(t\) isn't between min_t and max_t the hit is either behind the camera, too far away, or farther than something we already found, so no hit again. If it passes all of that, I shrink max_t to this \(t\), blend the three vertex normals using those same corner weights so the surface shades smoothly (and normalize it, since blending unit vectors doesn't give you a unit vector), and save \(t\), the primitive, and its BSDF.

Part 2: Bounding Volume Hierarchy

BVH construction

The idea is that if a ray misses a box, it can't hit anything inside the box, so we can skip all of it at once. I build the tree recursively. For a group of primitives I make two boxes: one around all the primitives (that's the node's box), and one around just their centroids. If the group has max_leaf_size or fewer primitives, it's a leaf and its start and end iterators point at that group. Otherwise I split it. I pick whichever axis the centroid box is longest on and split at the middle of the centroid box on that axis. Then partition shuffles the primitives in place so everything on the left of the split comes first, and I recurse on each half. Since everything gets reordered in place, a leaf is just a range in the same vector, no new lists needed.

Splitting heuristic: I use the middle of the centroid box instead of the middle of the full box, so the split always lands somewhere between the smallest and biggest centroids. Longest axis because that's usually where things are the most spread out. The spec warned that if all the centroids end up on the same side, the recursion gets the exact same group forever and crashes. So if that happens I fall back to splitting at the median with nth_element, which always gives two non-empty halves.

BVH intersection

For ray vs. box I used the slab method. I find the t on each axis where the ray crosses the two planes of the box. The ray is only inside the box when it's inside all three slabs at once, so that's from the latest time it enters to the earliest time it leaves. If that's empty, or doesn't overlap the ray's [min_t, max_t], it's a miss.To traverse the tree I check the node's box first and skip the whole subtree if the ray misses. At a leaf I test every primitive. For intersect I have to visit both children, because the closest hit could be in either one, but since every primitive lowers code>max_t when it hits, the farther hits just get thrown out on their own. For has_intersection (which is used for shadow rays) I don't care which hit is closest, so I just return the moment I find one.

Part 3: Direct Illumination

Uniform hemisphere sampling

First the diffuse BSDF, which is about two lines. A diffuse surface scatters light the same in every direction, so its BSDF is just a constant, \(f = \frac{\text{albedo}}{\pi}\). The \(\frac{1}{\pi}\) is there so the surface doesn't give off more light than it gets.Now to estimate the light at a hit poin I use a local frame where the normal points straight up , which is nice because then \(\cos\theta\) is just the z of a direction. I pick \(N\) random directions evenly over the hemisphere, each with probability \(p = \frac{1}{2\pi}\), and shoot a ray out each way with min_t = EPS_F so the ray doesn't just hit the surface it started on. If it hits something, that thing's emission is the incoming light, and I plug it into the Monte Carlo estimate:

\[ L_{out} \approx \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=1}^{N} \frac{f(\omega_j \to \omega_r) \, L_i(\omega_j) \cos\theta_j}{p(\omega_j)} \]

Different parts want different frames her: the BSDF and \(\cos\theta\) want the local frame, but the ray has to be shot in world space, so the direction gets converted with o2w before tracing.

Importance sampling the lights

Instead of shooting random directions and hoping one hits a light, why not just aim at the lights? For every light I sample points on it with sample_L, which gives me the incoming light, the direction to the light, how far away it is, and the pdf. A point light only needs one sample since every sample would be the same anyway, and area lights get ns_area_light samples. If the light is behind the surface (\(\cos\theta \leq 0\)) useless, dont use it. Otherwise I shoot a shadow ray toward the light with max_t = distToLight - EPS_F so it stops just before the light itself. If nothing's in the way, I add the same term as before. I average each light's samples and then add all the lights together.

Hemisphere sampling vs. light sampling for 64 samples per pixel, 32 light rays

Soft shadows with different numbers of light rays (1 sample per pixel, light sampling)

With 1 light ray, every point in the shadow either sees the one spot on the light it picked or it doesn't, so the soft shadow under the bunny is this grainy mess of light and dark pixels. But when we go to 4, 16, and 64 light rays, each pixel averages over more spots on the light, and the shadow turns into a smoother fade. Kinda the same story as supersampling, more samples means a smoother blend. The noise drops roughly with the square root of the number of samples, which is also why the jump from 16 to 64 doesn't look as big as 1 to 4. Diminishing returns again!

Comparing the two methods

Both methods are estimating the same integral and they'll both get to the same image if you wait long enough, but light sampling is way less noisy at the same sample count, which is kinda the point since both are estimates. With hemisphere sampling, only the directions that happen to point at the small light on the ceiling add anything, so most samples come back as zero and the image ends up full of speckles. Plus hemisphere sampling also can't do point lights at all, since the chance of a random direction hitting one exact point is basically zero. Light sampling always aims at the lights, so every sample counts unless something is actually blocking it. The only real noise left is right at the shadow edges, so the surfaces come out clean and the soft shadows look smooth while the hemisphere version is still covered in static.

Part 4: Global Illumination

sample_f picks an incoming direction with cosine-weighted hemisphere sampling, which means it picks directions near the normal more often since those count more anyway. It stores the pdf (\(\frac{\cos\theta}{\pi}\)) and returns the BSDF valueNow the fun, est_radiance_global_illumination adds the zero-bounce light (looking straight at a light) and then calls at_least_one_bounce_radiance, which is the recursive one. I did it in four steps:

  1. Add the direct lighting at this hit point with one_bounce_radiance.
  2. If the ray's depth is 1, stop, we're out of bounces.
  3. Otherwise pick one new direction with sample_f and shoot a ray that way (min_t = EPS_F again, and depth goes down by 1).
  4. If it hits something, call the function again on that new hit point and add whatever comes back, weighted by \(\frac{f \cos\theta}{p}\).

Its important that the recursive call doesn't add the next surface's emission, because that light already got counted as direct lighting at the point before it, adding it again would count it twice. Camera rays start with a depth of max_ray_depth, and if isAccumBounces is false I only add direct lighting when depth hits 1, so the image shows just the \(m\)th bounce and nothing else. Russian Roulette is the fun one. I always trace the first bounce from the camera's hit point, but after that every path keeps going with probability 0.65 and stops with probability 0.35. If it keeps going, I divide its contribution by 0.65. That makes up exactly for the paths that got stopped, so on average the answer comes out the same,but long paths get way cheaper.

Direct illumination only vs. indirect illumination only (1024 samples per pixel)

The direct only image is super contrasty. Anything facing the light is bright, but the ceiling and the bottoms of the spheres are pitch black because the light can't reach them directly. The indirect only image is dimmer and much softer. It's the one lighting up the ceiling and the shadows, and it's where all the color bleeding comes from, red and blue from the walls spilling onto the spheres and floor. Add them together and you get the full image.

Only the \(m\)th bounce of light (CBbunny.dae, isAccumBounces = false, 1024 samples per pixel)

The 2nd bounce is light that already bounced off one surface before reaching the spot we're looking at. It's what lights up the ceiling, the underside of the bunny, and the shadow under it, and it carries the red and blue from the walls onto the bunny and floor. The 3rd bounce is dimmer and even smoother, filling in the corners and softening the shadows a bit more. Every bounce loses some light to absorption, so each one adds less than the one before. A rasterizer usually only does direct shading and fakes everything else with a flat ambient term, so this is the stuff it can't do: shadows that aren't pure black, corners that are a little darker, and walls tinting whatever's next to them. That's what makes the path traced image actually look real.

Accumulated bounces (CBbunny.dae, isAccumBounces = true, 1024 samples per pixel)

These are just running totals of the images above. The biggest jump by far is from \(m = 1\) (just direct lighting) to \(m = 2\), where the ceiling and shadows finally get filled in. After \(m = 3\) it's honestly hard to tell them apart, since each extra bounce adds so little light.

Part 5: Adaptive Sampling

What adaptive sampling is

Some pixels figure themselves out fast, like a flat wall facing the light, where every sample comes back about the same. Others need way more, like soft shadows and corners lit by bounced light. So why give every pixel the same number of samples? Adaptive sampling stops shooting rays through a pixel once we're pretty sure it's converged, so the samples go to the hard parts instead of being wasted on the easy ones. For each pixel I keep two running sums of each sample's illuminance and that's all I need to get the mean and variance without saving every sample:

\[ \mu = \frac{s_1}{n}, \quad \sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n - 1}\left(s_2 - \frac{s_1^2}{n}\right) \]

Checking after every single sample would be slow, so I split it into batces of samples and I compute

\[ I = 1.96 \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \]

which is the 95% confidence interval. If \(I \leq maxTolerance \cdot \mu\), it's converged and I stop. If not, I keep going until ns_aa samples. The pixel's color is the average of the samples I actually took, and I put that count in sampleCountBuffer so we get the sampling rate image (red means lots of samples, blue means few).

Results (2048 max samples per pixel, batch size 64, tolerance 0.05, 1 light ray, max ray depth 5)

The rate images are blue on the light and on the brightly lit surfaces, where every sample comes back almost the same so they converge right away. They're red in the soft shadows, on the ceiling, and in the corners, where most of the light comes from random bounced paths that vary a lot from sample to sample. Which is exactly where you'd want the extra samples to go in adaptive sampling
